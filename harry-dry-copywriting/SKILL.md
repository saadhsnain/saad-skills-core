# Harry Dry Copywriting Method

Write copy that can't be copied.

## Before Writing: Gather What You Need

**Do not attempt to write copy until you have enough concrete information to pass the three questions.**

If the user's request lacks specifics, ask clarifying questions. You need:

### 1. Concrete details (for visualization)

Ask until you have tangible, specific facts:
- "What's a specific moment when your customer uses this?"
- "What does your product physically look like / feel like / sound like?"
- "Give me a number, a place, a name—something I can picture."

**Red flag:** If all you have is abstract benefits ("saves time," "improves productivity," "better experience"), you cannot write visualizable copy. Push for specifics.

### 2. Falsifiable claims (for credibility)

Ask until you have provable facts:
- "What's a specific result a customer got?"
- "What can you do that's measurably true?"
- "What would you bet money on being true about your product?"

**Red flag:** If everything is subjective ("great quality," "amazing service"), you have nothing falsifiable. Push for facts.

### 3. Unique positioning (for differentiation)

Ask until you know what only they can say:
- "What do you do that competitors literally cannot claim?"
- "What's weird or unexpected about your product/audience?"
- "If I showed this copy to your competitor, could they sign it?"

**Red flag:** If the copy could work for any competitor, it's not unique enough. Push for specifics.

### Minimum information checklist

Before writing, confirm you have at least:
- [ ] One concrete, visual detail (a place, object, person, or moment)
- [ ] One falsifiable fact (a number, result, or verifiable claim)
- [ ] One unique angle (something no competitor can say)

If missing any, ask. Do not fill gaps with generic copy.

### Example dialogue

**User:** "Write a tagline for my productivity app"

**Claude:** "I need a few specifics before I can write something that sticks:
1. What's one concrete thing users do with it? (Not 'manage tasks'—something I can picture)
2. What's a real result someone got? (A number, a before/after)
3. What makes you different from Notion, Todoist, etc.? (Something only you can claim)"

## The Three Questions

Ask these for every sentence. Three yeses = you're onto something. Three nos = rewrite.

### 1. Can I visualize it?

Close your eyes. Can you see it? If not, zoom in until you reach something concrete.

**Abstract → Concrete ladder:**
- "Regain fitness" → "Getting off the couch" → "Running" → "Running 5K" → "Couch to 5K"
- "Pretty people in big cities" → "Supermodels in London"
- "Old people in non-big cities" → "Dads in Ohio"

**Test:** Can you drop it on your foot? If it fades away, it's abstract. If it hurts, it's concrete.

### 2. Can I falsify it?

Can this be proved true or false? Falsifiable claims put your head on the chopping block. Ears perk up.

**Don't talk. Point.**
- Don't say "intelligent" → Point to "reads on the tube"
- Don't say "good-looking" → Point to "6 foot 2, looks like Ryan Gosling"
- Don't say "great investment" → Point to the 50-year gold chart

**Facts guarantee you're saying something.** Most people say wallpaper, word-shaped air, balloon smoke. A fact is real.

### 3. Can nobody else say this?

Never write an ad a competitor can sign.

**Bad:** "Don't just get a job. Change an entire industry." (Any recruiter can say this)
**Good:** "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio." (Only New Balance)
**Good:** "Your car has 5 numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has 6." (Only Volkswagen)

## The Writing Process

### Step 1: Define the poles

What's the current attitude? What's the desired attitude? String the wire between them.

### Step 2: Answer three questions

1. **Who are you talking to?** Tourist on a street? 39-year-old Super Bowl viewer? User who's been on your app 6 months?
2. **What do you have to say?** Why did you start this business? What's broken that you're fixing?
3. **How will you say it well?** Rhythm, contrast, metonymy, alliteration.

### Step 3: Find the conflict

Draw a line down the page. Write opposites on each side:
- Throw money and pray | Learn copywriting
- Corporate way | Copywriting way
- 7 steps to prepare | 1 step (Volkswagen)

Every great story has a "but."

### Step 4: Rewrite 20+ times

You don't write simply. You rewrite simply.

Copy and paste. Try a different approach. Repeat. Show versions to someone. "That one's best. Use that sentence but put it in that one."

## Core Laws

### Kaplan's Law of Words

Any words not working for you are working against you.

**Harry's Law:** You're not taking Kaplan's seriously enough.

### The Burrito Test

A good paragraph should hold together like a burrito—throw it and it shouldn't fall apart mid-air. Pull out a sentence. Does the paragraph break? Good. Does it still work? That sentence shouldn't have been there.

### One Mississippi, Two Mississippi

Show your ad to someone. Count "One Mississippi, two Mississippi." Do they get it? If not, simplify.

### The Garden Walk Test

Walk away. Come back. Can you remove anything? No? It's done.

### Two-Line Maximum

No paragraph longer than two lines. Forces clarity. Gives lines room to breathe. Like monkey bars—easy to swing between.

## Techniques

### Parallelism

Create rhythm through repetition:
- "Raise twice the cash, hire twice the staff, spend twice as much on ads"
- "Who are you talking to? What are you saying? How are you saying it?"

### Metonymy

Substitute literal terms with visual ones:
- "1,000 songs in your pocket" (not "in your media player")

### Juxtaposition

Create tension through contrast:
- "Dating app designed to be deleted"
- "How can 2 months' salary last forever?"

### First line, second line

First line creates curiosity. Second line delivers the payoff:
- "It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad—the same time it takes a Model S to go from 0 to 60."

## Newsletter Intros

Time, place, what's happening. Fresh bread, warm to touch:

> Hey, it's Harry. It's 3:47 a.m. I'm in the big smoke alone. Laptop, green tea, looking at the whiteboard like a dozy dog. Six words on it. Five with lines through.

Don't look for cleverness. Look at reality.

## After Writing: Self-Check

Before presenting copy, run it through the three questions:

| Question | Test | If it fails |
|----------|------|-------------|
| Can I visualize it? | Close your eyes. Can you see it? | Zoom in. Replace abstracts with concretes. |
| Can I falsify it? | Is this provably true or false? | Add a fact. Point, don't talk. |
| Can nobody else say this? | Could a competitor sign this? | Find what's unique. Dig deeper. |

**If any answer is "no," do not present the copy.** Rewrite until all three pass, or go back to the user for more information.

### Common failure patterns

- **Generic benefits:** "Save time and boost productivity" → Fails all three. Ask for specifics.
- **Adjective soup:** "Innovative, seamless, powerful solution" → Fails all three. Point to facts instead.
- **Category claims:** "The best project management tool" → Fails uniqueness. Find what only they can say.

## What AI Lacks

Taste, conviction, experience. AI lays out words in a fashion quite literally from all the stuff laid out before. A good writer arranges words in ways that haven't been laid out before.

If you think AI will do a better job than you, it probably will. But it can't see anything and doesn't believe anything.
